# Excel Prior Art for a Consulting Workbook Library: What to Adopt, What to Build, and Where You're Wrong

## TL;DR
- **The invariant core is real and worth adopting verbatim, but your central hypothesis is only half-right.** Mechanical enforcement (linting, diffing, assertion tests, colour-coding by rule) will fix your *symptoms* — hidden scenario inputs and structural bloat — but the deeper cause is a missing *architectural* rule that no standard leaves to enforcement: assumptions must live in visible cells, and one artifact should not serve two audiences. Fix the architecture first; enforce it second.
- **Adopt, don't reinvent:** the FAST Standard (free, CC-BY 4.0, the closest thing to an authoritative published standard), the ICAEW Twenty Principles (free, governance framing to defend the investment upward), the Macabacus/IB colour convention (blue = input is the single most-recognised signal), and IBCS (free, CC-BY-SA, now the basis of ISO 24896:2026) for the executive/reporting layer. For tooling, the `formulas` library (Python, actively maintained) replaces your COM recalculation dependency; PerfectXL/OAK/xltrail cover linting and diffing.
- **There is no open-source "spec-compiles-to-workbook" framework that already does what you propose.** You are not reinventing a solved problem; you are building into a genuine gap. The right pattern is: declarative spec (your YAML/JSON) → openpyxl generation → headless recalculation with `formulas` or LibreOffice → assertion tests on named outputs → automated diff in CI.

## Key Findings

### The three-tier landscape is real, but the tiers are not equally authoritative
There is no single ISO-style body that governs "financial modelling in Excel." What exists is a spectrum from one genuinely open published standard (FAST) through professional-body guidance (ICAEW), certification schemes (FMI), firm methodologies (SMART/Mazars, Operis, PwC), a communication standard that in 2026 became a real ISO standard (IBCS → ISO 24896), a governance/regulatory layer (SR 11-7, EUC/SOX controls), and an academic evidence base (EuSpRIG/Panko). The single most common failure in this space — which the brief rightly anticipates — is describing a consultant's blog methodology as if it were an authoritative standard. Only FAST, ICAEW, IBCS/ISO 24896, and SR 11-7 have named maintaining bodies and published documents.

### The evidence base is strong enough to defend the investment, with honest caveats
Panko's synthesis of field audits is the citable core. His 2005 review of 13 studies (1995–2004) found an average cell error rate of 5.2% across 43 spreadsheets, and a spreadsheet error rate of 94% across 88 spreadsheets studied — meaning 94% contained at least one error. His field-audit summary notes the most recent audits found errors in at least 86% of spreadsheets audited. The dissenting datapoint is important for intellectual honesty: Powell & Baker's Dartmouth Tuck study of 50 operational spreadsheets (270,722 cells) found errors in 0.9% to 1.8% of formula cells depending on definition (0.87% for outright wrong results, 1.79% overall) — an order of magnitude below Panko's headline. The *direction* is unanimous across two decades and multiple independent researchers; the *magnitude* is contested and definition-dependent. Use ranges, not false precision, when defending the investment upward.

The documented public failures give you named, defensible anecdotes: the JPMorgan Task Force Report (16 January 2013) describes a VaR model that operated through a series of Excel spreadsheets completed manually by copying and pasting data between them, contributing to the roughly US$6.2 billion Chief Investment Office "London Whale" trading loss; the Reinhart-Rogoff *Growth in a Time of Debt* error (five countries excluded by a mis-dragged range, exposed by Herndon in 2013); and the Barclays-Lehman episode (hidden — not deleted — columns reappearing in a converted PDF, adding unwanted assets).

### Modern Excel features are a trap for your specific problem
`LET`/`LAMBDA`/dynamic arrays genuinely improve some things, but the brief's scepticism is correct: `LET` makes a long formula *more comfortable to write* without making the hidden scenario input visible on the sheet. It is a readability tool, not an auditability tool. Adopting them wholesale would let you write more sophisticated hidden complexity.

## Details

### Section 1 — The landscape, tier by tier

**Tier (a): Formal published standards with a named maintaining body**

- **FAST Standard.** Maintained by the FAST Standard Organisation (FSO), a UK not-for-profit membership company established 2011. The current standard document (version 02c, July 2019) is a rulebook covering workbook design, sheet organisation, calculation blocks, formula design ("rule of thumb": formulas no longer than a thumb held against the formula bar), labelling, links, and flags. **Licence: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International** — genuinely free and adoptable; the FSO formalised this via a Deed of Gift specifically so users need not fear lock-in to a proprietary system. This is the closest thing to an authoritative, *measurable* published standard: the FSO explicitly positions it so a model's compliance can be assessed, and offers a FAST Level 1 Certificate. **Actually used:** yes, widely, especially in project/infrastructure finance and by firms like F1F9. Confirmed. https://www.fast-standard.org/
- **ICAEW Twenty Principles for Good Spreadsheet Practice.** Maintained by ICAEW via its Excel Community Advisory Group. First issued 2013; fourth edition issued May 2024. Free, organised into planning/strategy, designing/building, and control/management. It is *not* a modelling standard — it is a higher-level governance and risk framework. **Actually used:** yes as a reference and discussion basis; deliberately non-prescriptive. It does not tell you how to build; it tells you what disciplines to have. Confirmed. Note: the widely repeated claim that ICAEW "officially recognised FAST as the first financial modelling standard" appears in secondary sources; ICAEW's own position is that it does not mandate any specific standard. https://www.icaew.com/technical/technology/excel/twenty-principles
- **IBCS (International Business Communication Standards).** Maintained by the not-for-profit IBCS Association; originated from Rolf Hichert's work. **Licence: Creative Commons BY-SA** — free. Version 1.0 published 2015. Critically, IBCS principles formed the basis of **ISO 24896 — Notation for Business Reporting**, published 11 June 2026 (Edition 1); per IBCS the standard is based mainly on the UNIFY and CHECK parts of the SUCCESS formula, and IBCS 2.0 was released the same day. This moves IBCS from "influential convention" firmly into tier (a). Relevant to you for the *reporting/executive* layer: standard scenario notation (AC/PL/BU/FC/PY), consistent variance handling, "don't cut axes." Confirmed and upgraded in status. https://www.ibcs.com/
- **SR 11-7 (Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management).** Issued April 2011 jointly by the US Federal Reserve (SR 11-7) and OCC (2011-12). Governs model risk at supervised banks and explicitly names spreadsheets/EUC as particularly prone to model risk. Three pillars: robust development/implementation/use; effective validation by independent parties; sound governance. Three validation activities: conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring (process verification and benchmarking), outcomes analysis (back-testing). **Actually used:** yes — binding supervisory guidance for US banks and the conceptual template EUC-control vendors sell against. Relevant *only if* clients are regulated financial institutions, but its validation vocabulary ("effective challenge," independent review) is worth borrowing. Confirmed. https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/sr1107.htm

**Tier (b): De facto industry convention**

- **SMART / Corality lineage.** SMART was the methodology originated by Corality; Corality was acquired by Mazars, so SMART is now effectively the Mazars financial modelling methodology. Convention, not open standard; training-and-services-gated. Confirmed but corrected: it is not a freely published rulebook the way FAST is.
- **Modano.** A commercial modular ("Lego-block") modelling add-in and content-management system for Excel, associated with the Spreadsheet Standards Review Board (SSRB). A *modularity* approach — models assembled from pre-built linked modules with integrity checks. Proprietary. Relevant as a conceptual alternative to hand-generation. https://www.modano.com/
- **Operis / Operis Analysis Kit (OAK).** OAK is a commercial Excel add-in, originally Operis's in-house model-audit tool, now the leading commercial audit/review add-in used in banks and public bodies. Operis's build approach makes heavy, systematic use of named ranges. OAK5 is current, with Map, Compare and Summarize tools. Commercial, 15-day free trial. Confirmed. https://www.operis.com/
- **Investment-banking / Macabacus colour convention.** The de facto IB standard: **blue font = hardcoded input, black font = formula referencing the same sheet, green font = link to another sheet, red = external/warning**. Macabacus (commercial, now owned by Corporate Finance Institute) codified and automates this via AutoColor. Convention, not published standard — but the *most widely recognised* visual language in finance. Confirmed.
- **PwC Global Financial Modelling Guidelines** (January 2020): 5 core principles plus 10 practical design guidelines. Firm-published convention.

**Tier (c): Individual practitioner opinion (influential, not authoritative)**

- **Michael Rees** — *Principles of Financial Modelling* (Wiley), academically rigorous, strong on model design and risk/simulation.
- **Danielle Stein Fairhurst** — *Financial Modeling in Excel For Dummies*, *Using Excel for Business and Financial Modelling*; runs Plum Solutions; widely read and practical.
- **John Tjia** — *Building Financial Models* (McGraw-Hill), a classic, older.
- **Simon Benninga** — *Financial Modeling* (MIT Press), the academic/quant classic, predates modern Excel entirely.
- **Liam Bastick** — *Financial Modelling using Dynamic Arrays*, the CRaFT methodology; Excel MVP; the most current voice on LET/LAMBDA/dynamic arrays.
- **Financial Modeling Institute (FMI)** certifications: **AFM** (Advanced Financial Modeler, US$725 sit fee, four-hour proctored build-a-three-statement-model exam, no template, four sittings a year — 2026 dates 21 Feb, 9 May, 11 Jul, 24 Oct), **CFM** (Chartered, four-hour, annually in October, US$725), **MFM** (Master). Older sources cite US$695; re-enrolment is roughly US$300–350. These examine *modelling ability under time pressure*, not a specific published standard. Credentials for people, not a rulebook to adopt. Confirmed. https://fminstitute.com/

**What the brief missed:** IBCS's elevation to ISO 24896:2026; the **BEST Framework** (2025, thebestmodeling.org), a new attempt to codify dynamic-array/LAMBDA-era practice with a two-tier prescriptions-versus-recommendations structure; the EUC-governance vendor ecosystem (CIMCON, Apparity, Workscope, Mitratech/ClusterSeven), where SOX and SR-11-7 spreadsheet controls actually live in practice; and academic error-detection tools (ExceLint, which independently reproduced the Reinhart-Rogoff audit findings).

### Section 2 — What makes it elite

**The invariant core (agreed by every serious standard):**
1. **Separate inputs, calculations, and outputs** — physically and visibly.
2. **One formula per row, copied consistently across the time axis** — no different mechanics in adjacent columns.
3. **A consistent time axis** across all sheets (same column = same period everywhere).
4. **Short, simple, transparent formulas** (FAST's rule of thumb; break complex logic into steps).
5. **No hardcoded numbers buried in formulas** — inputs are visible, labelled cells.
6. **Colour/formatting to distinguish cell types** (input vs formula vs link).
7. **Every calculation has a unique, descriptive label.**
8. **Consistent, documented structure and navigation** (sheets ordered by category).

**Where standards genuinely disagree:**
- **Named ranges.** Operis embraces them heavily; FAST is sceptical ("named ranges by exception") because they hide references and defeat traceability — your `FTE_TARGET` example is *exactly* the failure FAST warns about.
- **Sign convention.** FAST uses "positive as normal" (inflows and outflows both positive in calc sheets); other conventions carry signs. Genuine disagreement.
- **Cell-by-cell vs top-down audit.** Model auditors disagree whether forensic cell-by-cell review or performance/analytical review is correct; most combine them.
- **Modularity.** Modano's module-assembly ideal versus FAST's flat-transparency ideal.

**Auditability vs maintainability vs communication (these conflict):**
- *Auditability* wants short formulas, no named ranges, visible intermediates, cell-by-cell traceability.
- *Maintainability* wants DRY (compute the denominator once, not per row), named ranges, modularity — the opposite of some auditability rules.
- *Communication* wants aggregation, hidden detail, colour, narrative — which fights auditability's show-everything instinct.

Your `=IF(SUM($I$4:$I$15)=0,...)` cell fails all three simultaneously: it recomputes the denominator per row (maintainability), hides `FTE_TARGET` (auditability), and writes text into a currency column (communication, and it silently breaks downstream sums).

### Section 3 — What you can integrate or build on

**Free / openly licensed (adopt as-is):**
- FAST Standard (CC-BY 4.0) — adopt the rulebook; write your linter against it.
- ICAEW Twenty Principles (free) — adopt as governance framing.
- IBCS / ISO 24896 notation (IBCS is CC-BY-SA; the ISO text itself is a paid document, CHF 135) — adopt for the reporting layer.

**Copyrighted / certification-gated (learn, don't redistribute):** the Macabacus colour scheme (the *convention* is free to follow; the software is not), Modano, OAK, SMART/Mazars materials, FMI curricula, and the practitioner books.

**Tooling:**
- **Linting/audit:** PerfectXL (Risk Finder + Compare, commercial, developed with TU Delft, roughly 39–44 documented principles), Operis OAK5 (commercial), Spreadsheet Compare/Inquire (ships with Office Professional Plus). Academic: ExceLint.
- **Diffing:** Microsoft Spreadsheet Compare (free with Office Pro Plus, wireable into `git diff` via a batch wrapper); xltrail (commercial, web UI, Git integration) and its free open-source cousins **git-xl** and **git-xltrail** (VBA-aware diff; worksheet diffing on the roadmap); PerfectXL Compare.
- **Version control:** git-xl / git-xltrail make `git diff` and `git merge` meaningful for the VBA layer; for worksheet-level semantic diff you need xltrail (commercial) or Spreadsheet Compare.
- **EUC governance (enterprise):** CIMCON, Apparity, Workscope, Mitratech/ClusterSeven — inventory, cell-level audit trail, sign-off workflow, SR-11-7 alignment.

**Is there an existing framework that already does what you propose?** No. There is no mature open-source "declarative spec → workbook" compiler. `xltpl` (openpyxl + Jinja2, MIT) is template-*fill*, not model-*compilation*. `xlrenderer` and `excel_exporter` are YAML-driven but niche, Excel-bound, and explicitly unmaintained. openpyxl and xlsxwriter remain the actual scaffolds everyone builds on. Commercial "model as structured objects" platforms (Quantrix, Modano, Causal, Pigment, Row Zero) are the only mature realisations of the idea, and they are proprietary and not Excel-native. **Verdict: you are building into a real gap, not reinventing a wheel — proceed, but keep the spec thin and declarative.**

### Section 4 — Formatting, structure, colour

**Colour conventions and origins.** The dominant convention is the investment-banking font-colour scheme (blue input / black same-sheet formula / green cross-sheet link / red external), codified and automated by Macabacus. FAST and others also use fill-based conventions (shaded input cells). **Which signals "editable input" most reliably: blue font is the most universally recognised in finance and IB; fill-based (shaded cell) is more visible when screenshotted and more robust for non-expert executive audiences.** Best practice for your two-audience problem: use *both* redundantly (blue font AND a light fill on inputs), because meaning must never be encoded in colour alone.

**Accessibility.** The hard rule across serious standards: never encode meaning in colour alone (it fails for roughly 8% of men with red-green colour-blindness and dies in greyscale screenshots). Pair every colour signal with a second cue: a border, a fill, a text flag, or number formatting. IBCS's semantic notation is explicitly a *non-colour* channel (fill patterns, two-letter scenario codes). Use colour-blind-safe palettes and avoid red/green adjacency for good/bad.

**Layout.** Sheets ordered by category (FAST: Input → Foundation → Calculation → Presentation). Consistent time axis. One formula per row. Freeze panes on headers. Use grouping/outline rather than hidden rows (hidden content is a documented risk — Barclays-Lehman). Print and PDF fidelity: set print areas and page breaks deliberately.

**Surviving the screenshot.** This is your real delivery constraint and most standards ignore it. Concrete rules: (1) put a self-contained title, period label, units and currency in every screenshot-able block; (2) use fills, not just fonts (fonts vanish in low-res screenshots); (3) never rely on a cell comment or off-screen named range to explain a number — the number must carry its own provenance nearby; (4) design presentation blocks sized to a slide's aspect ratio; (5) put the key assumption *on the same sheet region* as the result it drives.

**Flagging.** Adopt an explicit flag taxonomy: input / calculation / same-sheet link / cross-sheet link / external reference / error / check. FAST models timing flags on a dedicated Time sheet; the IB scheme uses font colour; PerfectXL and OAK detect violations. Add a visible "check" row convention (checks that must equal zero or TRUE) — standard in project-finance models and directly applicable to your reconciliation tab.

### Section 5 — Best ways of working

**Elite workflow end to end:** specification (scope, inputs, outputs, checks agreed before building) → build to standard → self-review against checklist → independent review ("effective challenge," borrowed from SR 11-7) → sign-off with documented version → change control. Model-audit firms (Operis, Mazars, BDO, Grant Thornton) formalise this; project-finance deals often mandate an independent model audit before financial close.

**Version control for binary workbooks.** Honest answer: Git treats `.xlsx` as binary and cannot diff it natively. Options that work: (1) git-xl/git-xltrail for VBA-aware diff; (2) commit the *declarative spec* to Git as source of truth and treat the `.xlsx` as a build artifact — the single biggest win available to you, because it makes your pipeline diffable like real code; (3) xltrail or Spreadsheet Compare for worksheet-level semantic diff; (4) disciplined version-in-filename plus a version-history sheet.

**Build-from-spec.** Declarative spec → workbook *is* a recognised idea but has no mature open-source implementation. For your situation it pays off strongly *because you already generate from Python*. Keep the spec thin and declarative (structure, inputs, output definitions, checks), let openpyxl handle rendering, and treat the spec as the versioned source.

**Testing — your highest-leverage unadopted practice.** There *is* a real practice of assertion-based and golden-master spreadsheet testing, championed by Panko (logic inspection), Louise Pryor, and the FlyingKoala/xlcalculator ecosystem. Concretely: (1) declare expected outputs for known inputs and assert them after recalculation; (2) build check cells into the model that must be zero or TRUE (balance sheet balances, sources = uses, no `#REF!`); (3) golden-master testing — snapshot the full output of a reference scenario and assert nothing changes unexpectedly across versions; (4) invariant tests (margins between 0–100%, no negative headcount). Panko's key empirical finding: only cell-by-cell code/logic inspection has been *demonstrated* to catch errors effectively — so automated assertion tests supplement but do not replace human review.

**Model review checklist and order.** A professional review runs roughly: (1) mechanical checks (structure, colour-coding, format consistency, hidden sheets/rows, external links); (2) formula integrity (unique formulas, consistency across rows, no hardcodes); (3) logic and accounting checks (statements tie, roll-forwards reconcile, opening + movement = closing); (4) sensitivity and stress testing; (5) documentation. Published checklists to steal: the FAST rules themselves, PerfectXL's principles, Eloquens community checklists.

**Preventing drift** (your central problem): drift happens because nothing fails when a prose rule is violated. The fix is exactly your instinct — make violations *fail a build*: (a) a linter that rejects text-in-numeric-columns, hardcodes-in-formulas, off-sheet named-range inputs, and inconsistent row formulas; (b) assertion tests in CI; (c) automated diff on every version so unexpected structural change is visible; (d) a hard cap on sections-per-sheet enforced by the generator. Because you generate from Python, you can enforce most of this *at generation time* — the workbook literally cannot be built if the spec violates the rules.

### Section 6 — What you're not doing that would improve output

**Modern Excel features — a position.** `LET` improves readability by naming intermediates; `LAMBDA` enables reusable named logic; dynamic arrays reduce copy-paste error surface. But for *your* failure mode they are neutral-to-harmful: `LET` would let you name the intermediate but `FTE_TARGET` would still be invisible on the sheet, and `LAMBDA` creates a black box that the BEST Framework explicitly warns must be governed, tested and validated like code before use. **Position: adopt dynamic arrays selectively (they genuinely cut copy-paste errors on the time axis), but do NOT adopt `LET`/`LAMBDA` as a fix for transparency — they make hidden complexity more comfortable to write, which is the opposite of what you need.** Your architecture rule (inputs visible on-sheet) must sit *above* any formula-level feature.

**Formula transparency tooling.** Excel Labs / Advanced Formula Environment (AFE) — a free Microsoft Garage add-in (aka.ms/get-afe; Desktop/Web/Mac) — provides a real code editor for named formulas and LAMBDAs with syntax highlighting, inline errors, comments, indentation, and import/export of LAMBDA modules from GitHub gists. Useful *if* you commit to LAMBDA; otherwise peripheral. For self-documenting formulas: the `N()`-comment trick, native cell comments/notes, and dependency tracing (Excel's Trace Precedents, or OAK/PerfectXL dependency maps).

**Evaluating formulas outside Excel (directly relevant to your COM dependency).** Production-grade options, ranked:
- **`formulas`** (vinci1it2000) — the clear winner. Actively maintained (v1.3.4, March 2026), roughly 500 GitHub stars, full coverage of Excel financial functions (55/55) and around 90% overall, handles circular references, exports the model to JSON, reads via openpyxl, has a CLI and Flask API. **This can replace desktop-Excel-over-COM recalculation for most workbooks.** https://github.com/vinci1it2000/formulas
- **pycel** — compiles a workbook to a Python dependency graph, but the last release was 2021 (1.0b30) and it carries an **unpatched critical RCE: CVE-2024-53924 / GHSA-pw67-xjhq-389w** (published 17 April 2025, CVSS 9.8), where operating on an untrusted spreadsheet allows code execution via a crafted formula; NVD and Snyk note there is no fixed version. Avoid on any untrusted input.
- **xlcalculator** — modernised koala2, but the last PyPI release was February 2023, effectively discontinued; no array/CSE formula support; documented function-fidelity gaps (VLOOKUP exact-match only, LN differs from Excel).
- **koala2** — dead, superseded by xlcalculator.
- **LibreOffice headless** — the higher-fidelity fallback for workbooks using dynamic arrays or LAMBDA that pure-Python engines cannot handle. Use a `calculateAll()` UNO/Basic macro (don't rely on `--convert-to` alone, which needs "Recalculation on File Load = Always"). Caveat: documented financial-function discrepancies versus Excel, mostly around YEARFRAC and day-count basis (ODDL*, DB/DDB, XIRR/XNPV) — spot-check valuation outputs.

No pure-Python engine reliably handles Excel 365 dynamic arrays, LAMBDA or LET yet; for those, LibreOffice or Excel-via-COM remains necessary.

**Automated diffing for CI.** Wire Spreadsheet Compare (free, Office Pro Plus) or PerfectXL Compare into review; use git-xl for VBA; best of all, diff the *spec* and the recalculated *output values* in your pipeline so every change shows what moved numerically.

**AI-assisted modelling — known failure modes (2025–2026).** Recent benchmarks (SpreadsheetArena, BlueFin, MBABench, BankerToolBench, Finch) converge on consistent failure modes: (1) **hardcoding values instead of formulas** — flagged as unacceptable in IB contexts; even strong models do this; (2) **broken lookups at scale** — structurally correct Tables whose lookups fire error strings across whole columns; (3) **overwriting supplied scaffolding or branding** instead of augmenting it; (4) **material domain-logic errors** — applying P/E multiples to loss-making firms, adding cost synergies to the revenue line, reporting accretion when dilution is required; (5) **formula-reasoning errors** — failing to reconstruct latent business logic; (6) over-saturated styling. ICAEW's June 2026 guidance is explicit: treat any AI-generated model as a *draft to be checked* — verify all core sections are present, hunt for hidden sheets/rows/columns, remove unintended external links. **Control pattern:** constrain the AI to emit your *declarative spec* (which your generator validates and lints), never a finished workbook; then your existing pipeline enforces correctness.

### Section 7 — Critique and blind spots

**Where your approach is wrong or naive.** Mechanical enforcement is *necessary but not sufficient*. Your `FTE_TARGET` example is not fundamentally a formatting or linting failure — it is an *architecture* failure: you put a scenario driver in a named range instead of a visible input cell, and you presented three columns with different mechanics as if parallel. A linter can catch "off-sheet named range used as input" and "text in numeric column," but it cannot make you design a visible driver block. **The real problem is partly upstream of enforcement: a missing design rule and a missing spec discipline.** Enforce, yes — but first mandate: every scenario driver is a visible, labelled cell in a driver block; columns that use different mechanics are labelled as such or physically separated.

**Is mechanical enforcement the right bet?** Mostly yes, and it is the right *first* concrete build — because you generate from Python, you can enforce at generation time, which is stronger than post-hoc linting. But pair it with two non-mechanical things: a one-page design standard that fits on a screen (not your drifting methodology doc), and independent review on high-stakes models.

**The two-audience problem — the recognised pattern is separation, not one clever artifact.** Elite practice does *not* try to make one sheet serve both the executive and the auditor. The pattern is **model/report separation with progressive disclosure**: a thin presentation/report layer (IBCS-styled, screenshot-ready, narrative, assumptions summarised) sitting on top of a calculation engine the analyst audits. FAST bakes this into its Input → Foundation → Calculation → **Presentation** sheet ordering. Your instinct to combine them is the source of both symptoms (bloat and hidden logic). Split them.

**The explain-upward requirement.** If someone must defend a number without the model open, the workbook owes them a **methodology note / assumptions page / narrative layer**: a one-page, plain-language statement of the key drivers, the source of each material assumption, the calculation logic in words, and the scenario definitions. This is a recognised pattern — FMI's AFM explicitly tests the ability to explain what the numbers mean, ICAEW requires documentation, and a dedicated assumptions/cover sheet is standard practice. Build this as a generated artifact from your spec (the spec already knows the drivers), so it never drifts from the model.

**What you'll regret in 12 months:** (1) not committing the spec to version control from day one; (2) a linter with too many rules that people route around — start with 5–6 hard rules; (3) letting the report and engine re-merge under deadline pressure; (4) depending on COM recalculation when `formulas` would have decoupled you from desktop Excel; (5) not building the assumptions/methodology page as a first-class generated artifact.

**What you didn't ask that matters more:** (1) **Who owns the standard and how it changes** — a standard with no owner drifts regardless of enforcement; assign an owner and a change process. (2) **How you'll test the checks themselves** — a check cell wired wrong gives false comfort. (3) **Data lineage and provenance** — where each input came from and when, which matters more for explain-upward than formatting does. (4) **The reconciliation-against-source-of-truth tab** — arguably your highest-risk surface, deserving formal invariant tests.

## Recommendations

**Do this first (highest impact, low effort):** Split every workbook into a *calculation engine* and a *presentation/report layer*, and mandate that **every scenario driver is a visible, labelled input cell in a driver block** (never an invisible named range). This single architectural rule kills both your symptoms and requires no tooling. Effort: low. Impact: high.

**Then, staged:**
1. **Adopt FAST + ICAEW + IBCS as your written foundation** (all free). Distil to a one-screen internal standard. Effort: low. Impact: medium (stops methodology drift).
2. **Build generation-time enforcement into your Python pipeline** — 5–6 hard rules that fail the build: no text in numeric columns; no hardcodes in formulas; no off-sheet named-range inputs; consistent row formulas; cap sections-per-sheet; require a check row. Effort: medium. Impact: high. This is your mechanical-enforcement bet, done right.
3. **Replace COM recalculation with `formulas`** (pure Python, actively maintained), keeping LibreOffice-headless (`calculateAll()`) as fallback for LAMBDA/dynamic-array workbooks. Effort: medium. Impact: high (removes desktop-Excel dependency, enables CI).
4. **Add assertion and golden-master testing**: expected outputs for reference scenarios, check cells = 0/TRUE, snapshot diffs across versions. Effort: medium. Impact: high (your anti-drift ratchet).
5. **Commit the declarative spec to Git as source of truth; treat `.xlsx` as a build artifact.** Add automated output-value diffing per change. Effort: medium. Impact: high (real version control for a binary format).
6. **Adopt the IB/Macabacus colour convention with redundant fills** for accessibility and screenshot survival; generate the assumptions/methodology page from the spec. Effort: low-medium. Impact: medium-high.
7. **Reserve independent human review (SR-11-7-style effective challenge) for high-stakes models** — automated tests supplement but do not replace cell-by-cell logic inspection (Panko).

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:** if your workbooks routinely use Excel 365 dynamic arrays or LAMBDA, keep Excel/LibreOffice recalculation rather than betting on `formulas`. If clients are regulated banks, elevate SR-11-7/EUC governance from optional to mandatory. If more than two or three people build models, the standard needs a named owner and formal change control before enforcement will hold.

## Caveats
- **Confidence levels.** *Verified* (from primary/official sources): FAST licence, CC-BY status, version 02c July 2019; ICAEW 4th edition May 2024 and free status; IBCS CC-BY-SA and ISO 24896 published 11 June 2026; SR 11-7 April 2011 origin and its three validation activities; FMI AFM/CFM US$725 sit fee and 2026 exam dates; `formulas`/pycel/xlcalculator maintenance status and dates and the pycel CVE; Macabacus colour convention; git-xl/xltrail/PerfectXL/OAK existence and function. *Believed* (consistent across multiple sources but definition-dependent): the Panko error-rate figures and the contrasting Powell-Baker figures — both are real published findings that disagree on magnitude. *Inferred* (analysis, not a cited standard): the specific "do this first" architectural prescription, the two-audience separation recommendation, and the generation-time-enforcement design.
- **"Standards" that may not be authoritative.** SMART, Modano, PwC guidelines, Macabacus colours, and the BEST Framework are *conventions or firm/individual methodologies*, not authoritative published standards — do not present them upward as if they were. The only items with genuine standard-body standing are FAST (CC-BY, FSO), ICAEW's principles (professional body), IBCS/ISO 24896 (ISO), and SR 11-7 (US regulators).
- **Recency.** IBCS → ISO 24896 and IBCS 2.0 are 11 June 2026 developments; the ICAEW 20 Principles 4th edition is May 2024; much canonical modelling literature (Tjia, Benninga, and even parts of FAST 02c from 2019) predates dynamic arrays and LAMBDA, so treat its formula-level advice as pre-modern-Excel. The AI failure-mode evidence is 2025–2026 and evolving fast.
- **The evidence base has critics.** The Panko/EuSpRIG error-rate literature is challenged on small sample sizes and inconsistent error definitions; Powell et al.'s larger, more recent sample found materially lower cell error rates. The direction is unanimous; the precise magnitude is not. Use ranges, not false precision, when defending the investment upward.
